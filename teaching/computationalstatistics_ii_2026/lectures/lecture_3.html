Feature regression only needs certain inner products

For a fixed map \(\phi:\X\to\R^m\), let \(\Phi_{ij}=\phi_j(x_i)\).

Feature ridge regression

\[\widehat w=\arg\min_{w\in\R^m} \left\{\frac1n\|y-\Phi w\|_2^2+\lambda\|w\|_2^2\right\}, \qquad \lambda>0.\]

The normal equations give \((\Phi^\top\Phi+n\lambda I_m)\widehat w=\Phi^\top y\).

Proposition 1 (Primal and dual forms) \[\widehat w=\Phi^\top\alpha,\qquad (\Phi\Phi^\top+n\lambda I_n)\alpha=y.\]

Prediction uses only \(K(x,z)=\phi(x)^\top\phi(z)\).

Verify the statement by multiplying Phi^T alpha by the primal normal-equation matrix. Both regularised matrices are positive definite. With row vectors phi(x_i)^T in Phi, Phi Phi^T is n by n, whereas Phi^T Phi is m by m. No intercept is included here; it is treated separately in the appendix.

What is a kernel?

Definition 1 (Positive semidefinite kernel) A function \(K:\X\times\X\to\R\) is a kernel if it is symmetric and

\[\sum_{i,j=1}^n a_i a_jK(x_i,x_j)\geq0\]

for every \(n\geq1\), every \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\in\X\) and every \(a\in\R^n\).

  • The Gram matrix is \(G=(K(x_i,x_j))_{i,j=1}^n\); the condition is \(G\succeq0\).
  • Many books call this a positive definite kernel, allowing equality.
  • Strict positive definiteness requires \(a^\top Ga>0\) for \(a\ne0\) and distinct inputs.
  • A kernel may take negative values. Its Gram matrices must satisfy a quadratic inequality.

A similarity score need not be a kernel

On \(\X=\{a,b\}\), consider

\[K(a,a)=K(b,b)=1,\qquad K(a,b)=K(b,a)=2.\]

\[G=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \begin{pmatrix}1&-1\end{pmatrix}G \begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}=-2<0.\]

  • Symmetry, nonnegative entries and a positive diagonal are insufficient.
  • A valid kernel necessarily satisfies \(|K(x,z)|^2\leq K(x,x)K(z,z)\).
  • Checking a few Gram matrices can disprove validity; it does not establish validity on an entire domain.

The inequality follows from the determinant of the 2 by 2 positive semidefinite Gram matrix. Later it also follows from Cauchy–Schwarz in the RKHS.

Constructing kernels

Proposition 2 (Kernel algebra) If \(K_1,K_2\) are kernels, \(a\geq0\), \(h:\X\to\R\), and \(T:\mathcal Z\to\X\), each of the following defines a kernel:

  1. Sum: \(K=K_1+K_2\).
  2. Nonnegative scaling: \(K=aK_1\).
  3. Product: \(K(x,y)=K_1(x,y)K_2(x,y)\).
  4. Multiplication by a function: \(K(x,y)=h(x)K_1(x,y)h(y)\).
  5. Composition: \(K(u,v)=K_1(T(u),T(v))\) on \(\mathcal Z\).
  6. Pointwise limit: \(K(x,y)=\lim_{m\to\infty}K_m(x,y)\), provided each \(K_m\) is a kernel and the limit exists and is finite for every \(x,y\in\X\).

Why? Gram matrices use sums, entrywise products, or \(DGD\); alternatively, construct feature maps.

For the product, cite the Schur product theorem or use tensor-product features. For finite-dimensional features, coordinates are phi_j(x) psi_k(x). For a pointwise limit, every finite quadratic form is a limit of nonnegative numbers. Here ‘finite’ means the limiting values are real, not infinity. The coefficients in a positive combination must be nonnegative; h itself need not be.

  • For the product, factor the two Gram matrices as \(G_1=BB^\top\) and \(G_2=CC^\top\), and construct vectors whose inner products give \(K_1(x_i,x_j)K_2(x_i,x_j)\).
  • For the pointwise limit, justify passing the limit through a finite quadratic form.

Allow about 5 minutes for selected properties; leave the others for independent work. Symmetry holds in all six cases. Sum and scaling preserve nonnegative quadratic forms. For the product, let v_i have coordinates B_{ip}C_{iq}; then v_i^T v_j=(G_1){ij}(G_2){ij}, so the new Gram matrix is positive semidefinite. Multiplication by h gives D G_1 D with D_{ii}=h(x_i). Composition uses the Gram matrix at the transformed inputs T(u_i). For a pointwise limit, fix arbitrary n, inputs and coefficients: the finite sum converges to the limiting quadratic form, which is nonnegative because every quadratic form in the sequence is nonnegative.

Exercise: validity of the Gaussian kernel

Exercise 2 (The Gaussian kernel) Let \(\ell>0\). Prove that

\[K_\ell(x,z)=\exp\left\{-\frac{\|x-z\|_2^2}{2\ell^2}\right\}, \qquad x,z\in\R^d,\]

is a kernel, using the closure properties in Proposition 2.

For each M, the partial sum \[\sum_{r=0}^{M}\frac{(x^\top z)^r}{r!\ell^{2r}}\] is a kernel by the sum, scaling and product properties, starting from the linear kernel and the constant kernel 1. Its finite pointwise limit is \(e^{x^\top z/\ell^2}\). Finally, write \[K_\ell(x,z)=e^{-\|x\|^2/(2\ell^2)} e^{x^\top z/\ell^2}e^{-\|z\|^2/(2\ell^2)}\] and apply multiplication by a function with \(h(x)=e^{-\|x\|^2/(2\ell^2)}\). This avoids constructing an infinite feature vector explicitly.

An explicit feature map

For \(x,z\in\R\),

\[K(x,z)=(1+xz)^2 =\langle\phi(x),\phi(z)\rangle_{\R^3},\qquad \phi(x)=(1,\sqrt2 x,x^2)^\top.\]

Here the inner product on the right is the usual one on \(\R^3\).

  • A linear score in these features is a polynomial \(f(x)=a+bx+cx^2\).
  • Its feature-space squared norm is \(a^2+b^2/2+c^2\).
  • The factor \(\sqrt2\) changes the relative penalty on coefficients.
Function values do not determine regularisation by themselves

The same set of polynomials can carry different norms. The kernel records that choice of geometry.

The feature vectors span R^3 on the domain R, so their coefficient representation is unique.

2. RKHS: evaluation and smoothness

Why use a space of functions?

We wish to optimise

\[\text{data fit}(f)+\lambda\,\text{complexity}(f).\]

  • Linear structure: add predictors and multiply them by scalars.
  • Geometry: use an inner product and the norm it induces.
  • Limits: sequences of improving approximations should have a limit in the space.
  • Stable evaluation: a small change in norm gives a small change in \(f(x_i)\).
Two different continuity questions

Continuity of \(x\mapsto f(x)\) concerns the input. Continuity of \(f\mapsto f(x)\) concerns the function-space norm.

First: what is a vector space?

Definition 2 (Real vector space) A real vector space is a nonempty set \(V\) with addition \(u+v\in V\) and scalar multiplication \(au\in V\), for \(u,v\in V\) and \(a\in\R\), satisfying:

  1. Addition: \(u+v=v+u\) and \((u+v)+w=u+(v+w)\).
  2. Zero and inverses: there is \(0\in V\) with \(u+0=u\); each \(u\) has \(-u\) with \(u+(-u)=0\).
  3. Scalar multiplication: \(1u=u\) and \(a(bu)=(ab)u\).
  4. Distributivity: \(a(u+v)=au+av\) and \((a+b)u=au+bu\).

The elements need not be coordinate vectors: functions can be vectors.

Functions as vectors

Let \(V=C[0,1]\), the set of continuous real-valued functions on \([0,1]\).

Pointwise operations

\[(f+g)(t)=f(t)+g(t),\qquad (af)(t)=af(t).\]

  • These operations preserve continuity, so their outputs remain in \(V\).
  • The zero vector is the function \(t\mapsto0\); the additive inverse is \(-f\).
  • The vector-space identities hold because they hold at each \(t\) in \(\R\).
  • The set \(\mathcal P_m\) of polynomials of degree at most \(m\) is another example.
Subspace

A nonempty subset \(S\subseteq V\) is a linear subspace if \(af+bg\in S\) for all \(f,g\in S\) and \(a,b\in\R\).

An inner product adds geometry

Definition 3 (Real inner product) An inner product on a real vector space \(V\) is a map \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\times V\to\R\) such that

  1. \(\langle au+bv,w\rangle=a\langle u,w\rangle+b\langle v,w\rangle\).
  2. \(\langle u,v\rangle=\langle v,u\rangle\).
  3. \(\langle u,u\rangle\geq0\), with equality if and only if \(u=0\).

Examples:

  • On \(\R^d\): \(\langle u,v\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^d u_jv_j\).
  • On \(C[0,1]\): \(\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1 f(t)g(t)\,dt\).

The last condition distinguishes an inner product from a merely positive semidefinite form.

Symmetry and linearity in the first argument imply linearity in the second. For continuous f, integral f^2=0 forces f=0 everywhere: any nonzero value would, by continuity, give a neighbourhood with a strictly positive integral. For arbitrary measurable functions this argument fails at individual points; we return to equality almost everywhere when discussing L2.

From an inner product to a norm

Definition 4 (Induced norm) The norm induced by an inner product is \[\|u\|=\sqrt{\langle u,u\rangle}.\]

It satisfies the three norm properties:

  1. \(\|u\|\geq0\), and \(\|u\|=0\) if and only if \(u=0\).
  2. \(\|au\|=|a|\,\|u\|\).
  3. \(\|u+v\|\leq\|u\|+\|v\|\) (triangle inequality).
Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

\[|\langle u,v\rangle|\leq\|u\|\,\|v\|.\]

For the integral inner product, \(\|f\|_2=(\int_0^1|f(t)|^2\,dt)^{1/2}\).

For v nonzero, expand ||u-tv||^2>=0 and minimise over the real scalar t to obtain Cauchy–Schwarz. Then expand ||u+v||^2 and apply this inequality to obtain the triangle inequality. This establishes the norm properties; do not assume them solely from positive definiteness.

A metric measures distance

Definition 5 (Metric space) A metric space is a nonempty set \(M\) equipped with a map \(d:M\times M\to[0,\infty)\) satisfying, for all \(u,v,w\in M\):

  1. \(d(u,v)=0\) if and only if \(u=v\).
  2. \(d(u,v)=d(v,u)\).
  3. \(d(u,w)\leq d(u,v)+d(v,w)\).

Every norm gives a metric:

\[d(u,v)=\|u-v\|.\]

A general metric space need not have addition, scalar multiplication or an inner product.

Convergence in a metric space

Definition 6 (Convergence) A sequence \((u_m)\) in \((M,d)\) converges to \(u\in M\) if \[\forall\varepsilon>0\quad\exists N\quad\forall m\geq N: \qquad d(u_m,u)<\varepsilon.\]

  • We write \(u_m\to u\) or \(d(u_m,u)\to0\).
  • The limit must belong to the specified space \(M\).
  • With an induced metric, convergence means \(\|u_m-u\|\to0\).
  • Convergence depends on the metric; for functions, it need not mean convergence at every input.

For example, \(d_2(f,g)=(\int_0^1|f-g|^2)^{1/2}\) measures an integrated discrepancy, while \(d_\infty(f,g)=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}|f(t)-g(t)|\) measures the largest discrepancy on \(C[0,1]\).

Cauchy sequences: compare terms with one another

Definition 7 (Cauchy sequence) A sequence \((u_m)\) in \((M,d)\) is Cauchy if \[\forall\varepsilon>0\quad\exists N\quad\forall m,k\geq N: \qquad d(u_m,u_k)<\varepsilon.\]

  • All sufficiently late terms must be close to one another.
  • This definition does not require a candidate limit.
  • Every convergent sequence is Cauchy, by the triangle inequality.
  • Requiring only \(d(u_{m+1},u_m)\to0\) is insufficient.
Compare the two conditions

\[\text{Convergent: }d(u_m,u)\to0. \qquad \text{Cauchy: }\sup_{m,k\geq N}d(u_m,u_k)\to0\quad(N\to\infty).\]

Completeness is a property of the metric space

Definition 8 (Complete metric space) A metric space \((M,d)\) is complete if every Cauchy sequence in \(M\) converges to an element of \(M\).

  • Completeness concerns every Cauchy sequence, not just a chosen example.
  • A Cauchy sequence may approach a point excluded from the space.
  • Both the set and the metric are part of the statement.
A familiar complete space

\(\R\) with \(d(x,y)=|x-y|\) is complete. More generally, \(\R^d\) with Euclidean distance is complete.

A Cauchy sequence can have a missing limit

In \(M=(0,1)\) with the usual distance, let \(u_m=1/(m+1)\), \(m\geq1\).

\[|u_m-u_k|\leq\frac1{N+1}\quad(m,k\geq N), \qquad u_m\longrightarrow0\text{ in }\R.\]

Since \(0\notin(0,1)\), this space is not complete. The sequence has no alternative limit inside \((0,1)\), since metric limits are unique.

Now: a Hilbert space

Definition 9 (Hilbert space) A Hilbert space is a vector space \(\H\) with an inner product such that the induced metric \[d(f,g)=\hnorm{f-g}=\sqrt{\ip{f-g}{f-g}}\] makes \(\H\) a complete metric space.

  • Equivalently, every norm-Cauchy sequence has a norm limit in \(\H\).
  • \(\R^d\) with its usual inner product is a Hilbert space.
  • The polynomial space in Exercise 6 has an inner product but is not a Hilbert space.
The three ingredients

\[\text{Hilbert space} =\text{vector space}+\text{inner product}+\text{completeness}.\]

Example: \(L^2[0,1]\)

Example 1 (Square-integrable functions) Informally, \(L^2[0,1]\) consists of real-valued functions on \([0,1]\) for which \[\int_0^1|f(t)|^2\,dt<\infty.\]

Its inner product and norm are \[\langle f,g\rangle_{L^2}=\int_0^1 f(t)g(t)\,dt, \qquad \|f\|_{L^2}=\left(\int_0^1|f(t)|^2\,dt\right)^{1/2}.\]

This space is complete for the induced metric, so it is a Hilbert space.

  • Every continuous function on \([0,1]\) belongs to \(L^2[0,1]\).
  • Discontinuous functions can also belong: for example, \(f(t)=\mathbf1_{[1/2,1]}(t)\).
  • A Cauchy sequence in this norm has an \(L^2\) limit, which need not be continuous.

Keep this introduction informal, as requested; leave the measure-theoretic details to oral explanation. Strictly, functions are measurable and are identified when they agree almost everywhere, so the integral form is positive definite on the resulting space. Do not introduce equivalence classes on the student slide. State completeness without proving it. The polynomial example illustrates why restricting to a smaller function space may lose completeness, even with the same integral norm.

The Hilbert-space toolkit

We will use two consequences of completeness:

  1. Orthogonal projection: for a closed linear subspace \(S\), each \(f\in\H\) has a unique decomposition \(f=f_S+f_\perp\), with \(f_S\in S\) and \(f_\perp\perp S\).
  2. Riesz representation: every bounded linear functional \(L:\H\to\R\) has a unique representer \(h_L\in\H\) such that \(L(f)=\ip f{h_L}\).

A subspace is closed if it contains the limits of its convergent sequences. Finite-dimensional subspaces are always closed.

These are stated as tools, not presumed prerequisites. A linear functional is a linear map from H to R; boundedness means |L(f)|<=C||f|| for a fixed finite C and every f. This is equivalent to continuity for a linear map. Projection comes from minimising distance to S; the parallelogram identity makes a minimising sequence Cauchy. Riesz can be motivated by projecting onto the orthogonal complement of ker L. No general functional-analysis proof is needed in this course. A pre-Hilbert space alone does not provide these tools.

An integral norm does not control a point value

On continuous functions on \([0,1]\), use \(\|f\|_{L^2}^2=\int_0^1 f(t)^2\,dt\).

Set \(g_m(t)=\max\{1-m|t-1/2|,0\}\), \(m\geq2\).

\[g_m(1/2)=1,\qquad \|g_m\|_{L^2}^2=\frac{2}{3m}\longrightarrow0.\]

Thus evaluation at \(1/2\) is not continuous for this norm.

An integral norm can make two functions close while their values at a chosen input remain far apart. An RKHS will require stable pointwise evaluation.

Separate the two issues: continuous functions with the integral norm give well-defined but unbounded evaluations; completing to L2 gives equivalence classes for which changing one value changes no element of L2. Do not claim C[0,1] with this norm is Hilbert.

Stable evaluation defines an RKHS

Definition 10 (Reproducing kernel Hilbert space) A Hilbert space \(\H\) of pointwise defined functions \(f:\X\to\R\) is an RKHS if, for every \(x\in\X\), the evaluation functional

\[E_x:\H\to\R,\qquad E_x(f)=f(x)\]

is bounded: there exists \(C_x<\infty\) such that \(|f(x)|\leq C_x\hnorm f\) for all \(f\in\H\).

  • \(C_x\) may depend on \(x\).
  • Boundedness is equivalent to continuity because \(E_x\) is linear.
  • Norm convergence then implies pointwise convergence.

Riesz produces the reproducing kernel

Apply Riesz to each \(E_x\). There is a unique \(K_x\in\H\) such that

Reproducing property

\[f(x)=\ip f{K_x},\qquad f\in\H.\]

Define \(K(x,z)=\ip{K_x}{K_z}\); then \(K_x(t)=K(t,x)\).

Proposition 3 (Norm of evaluation) \[\|E_x\|=\hnorm{K_x}=\sqrt{K(x,x)},\qquad |f(x)|\leq\hnorm f\sqrt{K(x,x)}.\]

Why is \(K\) a kernel? \(\sum_{i,j}a_ia_jK(x_i,x_j)=\hnorm{\sum_i a_iK_{x_i}}^2\geq0\).

Cauchy–Schwarz gives the bound. For K_x nonzero equality is attained by f=K_x/||K_x||; when K_x=0 both operator and representer have norm zero. The notation K_x denotes a function of t, not a scalar.

Conversely: a kernel determines an RKHS

Theorem 1 (Moore–Aronszajn) For every symmetric positive semidefinite kernel \(K\), there is a unique RKHS of functions on \(\X\) with reproducing kernel \(K\).

Its finite linear combinations of kernel sections are dense: \[\H_K=\overline{\operatorname{span}\{K_x:x\in\X\}}.\]

  • Canonical features: \(\phi(x)=K_x\in\H_K\).
  • Kernel trick: \(K(x,z)=\ip{\phi(x)}{\phi(z)}\).
  • The full RKHS can be infinite-dimensional; a training set supplies only finitely many sections.

Uniqueness is as a function space with its inner product, not just up to an unspecified dimension. Different feature realisations can contain unused orthogonal directions; the canonical RKHS is the closed span of its sections. Do not confuse the full space with the span at the observed training inputs.

Where completeness enters the construction

Start with \(f=\sum_i a_iK_{x_i}\) and \(g=\sum_j b_jK_{z_j}\). Set

\[\ip f g=\sum_{i,j}a_ib_jK(x_i,z_j).\]

  1. Positive semidefiniteness gives a nonnegative squared norm.
  2. The reproducing identity and Cauchy–Schwarz give \(|f(x)|\leq\hnorm f\sqrt{K(x,x)}\).
  3. A zero-norm combination is zero at every \(x\); different representations define the same inner product.
  4. Complete this space. The bound makes each point value converge along Cauchy sequences.

The completion is therefore still a space of actual functions.

One can begin with formal finite sums and quotient the null space. The semidefinite bilinear form satisfies Cauchy–Schwarz by the quadratic discriminant argument. A formal sum represents the zero function iff it has zero norm: the reverse implication uses ||f||^2=sum_i a_i f(x_i). In the completion, an element zero at every x is orthogonal to the dense span of all sections and hence is zero. This establishes that no additional nonzero completion elements have all evaluations zero. Uniqueness follows by the isometry on finite sums and density in any RKHS with this kernel.

The familiar kernel \(\min(s,t)\): specify the space carefully

Definition 11 (An anchored Sobolev space) Let \[\H=\left\{f:[0,1]\to\R: f(t)=\int_0^t u(r)\,dr\ \text{for some }u\in L^2[0,1]\right\},\] with \(\ip f g=\int_0^1 f'(r)g'(r)\,dr\).

  • Every \(f\) is absolutely continuous, \(f(0)=0\), and \(f'=u\) almost everywhere.
  • Conversely, these three properties characterise this space.
  • \(\hnorm f^2=\int_0^1|f'(r)|^2\,dr\) penalises variation.
  • The boundary condition is essential: constants would otherwise have zero norm.

Explain absolute continuity through the integral representation rather than presuming a strong command of measure theory. A derivative exists almost everywhere and recovers f by integration. Merely requiring continuous f with an a.e. square-integrable derivative is insufficient: singular functions can have derivative zero a.e. without being constant. We deliberately avoid writing the more customary H_0^1 because it often imposes both endpoints.

This space really is complete

The map

\[D:\H\to L^2[0,1],\qquad Df=f'\]

is a linear isometric bijection, with inverse \(u\mapsto\int_0^{\cdot}u(r)\,dr\).

Transfer completeness from L²

If \((f_m)\) is Cauchy in \(\H\), then \((f_m')\) is Cauchy in \(L^2\). Let \(f_m'\to u\) in \(L^2\), and define \(f(t)=\int_0^t u(r)\,dr\). Then \(f\in\H\) and

\[\hnorm{f_m-f}=\|f_m'-u\|_{L^2}\longrightarrow0.\]

Also, by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\[|f(t)-f(s)|\leq\hnorm f\sqrt{|t-s|}.\]

Thus evaluation is bounded, and norm convergence implies uniform convergence here.

Compute its reproducing kernel

For \(s\in[0,1]\), let \(K_s(t)=\min(s,t)\). Then \(K_s\in\H\) and \(K_s'(r)=\mathbf1_{(0,s)}(r)\) almost everywhere.

\[\ip f{K_s}=\int_0^s f'(r)\,dr=f(s),\qquad K(s,t)=\min(s,t).\]

3. Representer theorem and kernel regression

Optimisation over a function space

Given \((x_i,y_i)_{i=1}^n\), consider

\[\min_{f\in\H}\left\{ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\ell(y_i,f(x_i)) +\lambda\hnorm f^2\right\},\qquad \lambda>0.\]

  • The loss sees only \(n\) point values.
  • The penalty sees the whole function.
  • \(\H\) need not be finite-dimensional.
  • Can this problem be reduced to finitely many coefficients without discarding its minimiser?

Separate what the observations can see

Let \(S_n=\operatorname{span}\{K_{x_1},\ldots,K_{x_n}\}\).

Since \(S_n\) is closed, \[f=f_\parallel+f_\perp,\quad f_\perp\perp S_n.\]

For each training input, \[f_\perp(x_i)=\ip{f_\perp}{K_{x_i}}=0.\]

Projection preserves every fitted value.

The representer theorem

Theorem 2 (Representer theorem) Let \(L:\R^n\to\R\) depend on \(f\) only through its training values, and let \(\Omega:[0,\infty)\to\R\) be strictly increasing.

If \[\min_{f\in\H}\{L(f(x_1),\ldots,f(x_n))+\Omega(\hnorm f)\}\] has a minimiser, every minimiser belongs to \(S_n\). Therefore it has a representation \[\widehat f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha_iK(x_i,x).\]

The theorem gives a form, not by itself existence or uniqueness.

Strict increase excludes any nonzero perpendicular component. If Omega is only nondecreasing, projecting a minimiser still produces a minimiser in S_n, but not every minimiser need lie there. No convexity of the loss is needed for this conditional statement. KRR existence and uniqueness are established below. The general result is discussed by Schölkopf, Herbrich and Smola (2001).

Proof: remove an invisible component

For \(f=f_\parallel+f_\perp\),

  1. \(f(x_i)=f_\parallel(x_i)\), so the loss is unchanged.
  2. Orthogonality gives \(\hnorm f^2=\hnorm{f_\parallel}^2+\hnorm{f_\perp}^2\).
  3. If \(f_\perp\ne0\), strict monotonicity makes the penalty smaller after projection.
Consequently

\[L(f_\parallel(x_1),\ldots,f_\parallel(x_n)) +\Omega(\hnorm{f_\parallel}) <L(f(x_1),\ldots,f(x_n))+\Omega(\hnorm f).\]

Thus a minimiser cannot have \(f_\perp\ne0\).

Why one coefficient per observation?

There are \(n\) candidate sections, but \(\dim S_n=\operatorname{rank}(G)\leq n\).

Kernel ridge regression

With squared loss, the finite-dimensional objective becomes

\[Q(\alpha)=\frac1n\|y-G\alpha\|_2^2+\lambda\alpha^\top G\alpha.\]

Indeed, \[\left\|\sum_i\alpha_iK_{x_i}\right\|_{\H}^2 =\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_jK(x_i,x_j)=\alpha^\top G\alpha.\]

  • The penalty is not generally \(\|\alpha\|_2^2\).
  • The first-order equation is \(G\{(G+n\lambda I)\alpha-y\}=0\).
  • If \(G\) is singular, we cannot cancel \(G\).

The finite reduction is valid for the infimum even before existence is known: every f has a projection in S_n with no larger objective. The next slide constructs a minimiser of the convex quadratic, thereby proving existence over the whole RKHS. Gradient is (2/n)G(G alpha-y)+2 lambda G alpha. The n factor comes from using an average loss rather than a sum.

A solution exists even when the Gram matrix is singular

Theorem 3 (Kernel ridge solution) For \(G\succeq0\) and \(\lambda>0\), a canonical coefficient vector is \[\alpha_\lambda=(G+n\lambda I)^{-1}y.\] It yields the unique function minimising the KRR objective: \[\widehat f_\lambda(x)=k_x^\top\alpha_\lambda, \qquad k_x=(K(x_1,x),\ldots,K(x_n,x))^\top.\]

  • \(G+n\lambda I\) is positive definite, hence invertible.
  • This vector satisfies the first-order equation; \(Q\) is convex.
  • In \(f\), the term \(\lambda\hnorm f^2\) is strictly convex, giving uniqueness.
  • Other coefficient vectors may describe the same unique function.

Coefficients and functions have different notions of uniqueness

If \(v\in\ker G\), then

\[\left\|\sum_i v_iK_{x_i}\right\|_{\H}^2=v^\top Gv=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sum_i v_iK(x_i,x)=0\ \text{for every }x.\]

  • All minimisers of \(Q\) have the form \(\alpha_\lambda+v\), \(v\in\ker G\).
  • Their predictions agree on all inputs, not merely the training sample.
  • At duplicate inputs the sections coincide, but singularity also occurs with finite feature maps.

For the converse, a coefficient displacement delta has zero quadratic curvature iff ||G delta||^2/n+lambda delta^T G delta=0, which is equivalent to delta in ker G. The canonical alpha may itself contain a component in ker G, coming from y; this component defines the zero function. There is no claim that the canonical alpha is the Euclidean minimum-norm representation.

Compute a factorisation, then solve

\begin{algorithm} \caption{Kernel ridge regression (average squared loss)} \begin{algorithmic} \Require n training pairs, kernel K, penalty λ > 0 \State Construct the Gram matrix G and add nλ to its diagonal to obtain A. \State Compute the Cholesky factorisation A = LLᵀ. \State Solve Lu = y by forward substitution. \State Solve Lᵀα = u by backward substitution. \State At a new input x, compute the vector of kernels against the training inputs. \Return Its dot product with α \end{algorithmic} \end{algorithm}
  • Dense fitting: \(O(n^2)\) storage and \(O(n^3)\) arithmetic.
  • Prediction at \(m\) new inputs: \(O(mn)\) kernel evaluations and arithmetic.
  • Do not explicitly form the inverse; use triangular solves.

Cholesky is well-defined in exact arithmetic because lambda>0. Very small lambda can still cause poor numerical conditioning. Adding ‘jitter’ changes the diagonal shift and should be reported; do not silently treat it as having no effect on the fitted model. For small finite feature dimension m, the primal m by m system may be cheaper than the dual n by n system.

The bandwidth and the penalty do different jobs

Three Gaussian KRR fits with a fixed sample: a narrow bandwidth, a wider bandwidth, and stronger regularisation.

  • Bandwidth \(\ell\): changes the kernel and its geometry.
  • Penalty \(\lambda\): controls the norm within the chosen RKHS.
  • For \(c>0\), replacing \(K\) by \(cK\) at fixed \(\lambda\) gives the same fit as using \(K\) with penalty \(\lambda/c\).

The scaling identity follows from c k_x^T(cG+n lambda I)^-1 y =k_xT(G+n(lambda/c)I)-1 y. Do not compare numerical lambda values across different kernel amplitudes without accounting for their scale.

kernelCholesky = (matrix) => {
  const n = matrix.length;
  const lower = Array.from({length: n}, () => Array(n).fill(0));
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
    for (let j = 0; j <= i; j++) {
      let entry = matrix[i][j];
      for (let k = 0; k < j; k++) entry -= lower[i][k] * lower[j][k];
      if (i === j) {
        if (!(entry > 0)) throw new Error("The regularised matrix is not numerically positive definite.");
        lower[i][j] = Math.sqrt(entry);
      } else lower[i][j] = entry / lower[j][j];
    }
  }
  return lower;
}

kernelTriangularSolve = (lower, response) => {
  const n = response.length;
  const intermediate = Array(n).fill(0), alpha = Array(n).fill(0);
  for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
    let value = response[i];
    for (let j = 0; j < i; j++) value -= lower[i][j] * intermediate[j];
    intermediate[i] = value / lower[i][i];
  }
  for (let i = n - 1; i >= 0; i--) {
    let value = intermediate[i];
    for (let j = i + 1; j < n; j++) value -= lower[j][i] * alpha[j];
    alpha[i] = value / lower[i][i];
  }
  return alpha;
}

4. Spectra, statistical error and approximation

Ridge is a spectral filter

Let \(G=U\operatorname{diag}(\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_n)U^\top\), with \(\mu_j\geq0\).

Fitted values at the training inputs

\[\widehat y=S_\lambda y,\qquad S_\lambda=G(G+n\lambda I)^{-1} =U\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{\mu_j}{\mu_j+n\lambda}\right)U^\top.\]

  • Directions with \(\mu_j\gg n\lambda\) are largely retained.
  • Directions with \(\mu_j\ll n\lambda\) are strongly shrunk.
  • Directions in \(\ker G\) are removed.
  • The ordering concerns kernel eigenvectors, which need not be ordinary Fourier frequencies.

Effective degrees of freedom

Definition 12 (Effective degrees of freedom of KRR) For a fixed kernel, design and \(\lambda\), \[\operatorname{df}(\lambda)=\operatorname{tr}(S_\lambda) =\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{\mu_j}{\mu_j+n\lambda}.\]

This is a sensitivity measure: \(\sum_i\partial\widehat y_i/\partial y_i\), not simply a count of fitted coefficients.

Bias and variance at a fixed design

Suppose \(y=f_X+\varepsilon\), with \(f_X=(f_0(x_1),\ldots,f_0(x_n))^\top\), \(\E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0\), and \(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I\).

Proposition 4 (Conditional error of the fitted mean) \[\frac1n\E[\|\widehat y-f_X\|_2^2\mid X] =\frac1n\|(S_\lambda-I)f_X\|_2^2 +\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\operatorname{tr}(S_\lambda^2).\]

  • The first term is squared bias; the second is variance.
  • This measures error for the mean at the observed inputs.
  • It is not yet an error bound for a new random input.
  • Hyperparameters are treated as fixed, rather than chosen using these same responses.

Expand (S-I)f_X+S epsilon; the cross term has conditional mean zero and E||S epsilon||2=sigma2 tr(S^T S). Symmetry gives S^2. No Gaussian noise assumption is needed. Prediction of independent noisy responses at the same design adds sigma^2 per observation. Using y-dependent hyperparameters would require accounting for the selection procedure.

From a matrix to an integral operator

To describe the kernel beyond one sample, choose a probability measure \(\rho\) on \(\X\) and define

\[(T_Kg)(x)=\int_\X K(x,z)g(z)\,d\rho(z).\]

  • \(T_K\) acts on \(L^2(\rho)\); \(G/n\) is its empirical analogue.
  • \(K\) describes the geometry; \(\rho\) describes where inputs occur.
  • The spectrum depends on both \(K\) and \(\rho\).
  • We now state a result with explicit compactness and continuity assumptions.

With the empirical measure, at the n sample indices the operator is represented by G/n. Repeated points give redundant coordinates; the nonzero spectra still agree. This is an analogy, not a claim that the finite-sample eigenvectors equal evaluations of population eigenfunctions.

Mercer’s theorem: features from eigenfunctions

Theorem 4 (Mercer expansion) Let \(\X\) be a compact metric space, \(\rho\) a Borel probability measure with full support, and \(K\) a continuous symmetric positive semidefinite kernel.

The positive eigenvalues \(\eta_j\) of \(T_K\) and continuous eigenfunctions \(e_j\), orthonormal in \(L^2(\rho)\), give \[K(x,z)=\sum_j\eta_j e_j(x)e_j(z).\] The series converges absolutely and uniformly on \(\X\times\X\).

  • There may be finitely or infinitely many positive eigenvalues.
  • The associated feature map is \(\phi(x)=(\sqrt{\eta_j}e_j(x))_j\in\ell^2\).
  • Zero-eigenvalue directions need not span part of the RKHS.

State this theorem without proof. Compactness and kernel continuity make T_K compact, positive and self-adjoint; full support ensures continuous eigenfunctions and the expansion describe the kernel on all of X, rather than just the support of rho. Positive-eigenvalue eigenfunctions form a basis of the closure of range T_K, not necessarily of all L2(rho). Do not apply this compact-domain statement directly to an unbounded domain.

The RKHS norm weights spectral coefficients

Under the assumptions of Theorem 4, write \(f=\sum_j b_je_j\).

\[f\in\H_K\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \sum_j\frac{b_j^2}{\eta_j}<\infty, \qquad \|f\|_{\H_K}^2=\sum_j\frac{b_j^2}{\eta_j}.\]

Here the expansion has no component in \(\ker T_K\) and defines its canonical continuous representative.

  • The functions \(\sqrt{\eta_j}e_j\) form an orthonormal basis of \(\H_K\).
  • Small \(\eta_j\) make a direction expensive in RKHS norm.
  • This explains why regularisation suppresses some directions before others.
  • Belonging to \(L^2(\rho)\) alone only requires \(\sum_j b_j^2<\infty\).

The iff is among functions with the stated positive-eigenfunction expansion, not all of L2 without excluding its null space. Full support identifies continuous representatives uniquely. Uniform convergence of f’s series follows from Cauchy–Schwarz and the uniformly vanishing diagonal Mercer tail. This gives a second analytic description of smoothness, in addition to derivatives.

The spectrum of \(\min(s,t)\)

For \(\rho\) uniform on \([0,1]\), the kernel has

\[e_j(t)=\sqrt2\sin\big((j-\tfrac12)\pi t\big),\qquad \eta_j=\frac{1}{(j-\tfrac12)^2\pi^2},\quad j\geq1.\]

Consequently, \[\hnorm f^2=\sum_{j\geq1}(j-\tfrac12)^2\pi^2 b_j^2 =\int_0^1|f'(t)|^2\,dt.\]

  • Higher-frequency components receive a quadratic penalty.
  • The boundary conditions are \(e_j(0)=0\) and \(e_j'(1)=0\).
  • The second condition arises from the operator eigenproblem, not from a requirement that every RKHS function have derivative zero at one.

Population ridge: the same filtering principle

Let \(Y=f_0(X)+\varepsilon\), \(X\sim\rho\), \(\E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0\), and \(\E[Y^2]<\infty\).

The population target is \[f_\lambda^{\mathrm{pop}}=\arg\min_{f\in\H_K} \{\E[(Y-f(X))^2]+\lambda\|f\|_{\H_K}^2\}.\]

If \(\theta_j=\langle f_0,e_j\rangle_{L^2(\rho)}\), then

\[f_\lambda^{\mathrm{pop}}=\sum_j \frac{\eta_j}{\eta_j+\lambda}\theta_j e_j.\]

Any component of \(f_0\) in \(\ker T_K\) is unlearnable in this space.

The irreducible noise term is constant in f. Minimise each (theta_j-b_j)^2+lambda b_j^2/eta_j. The result belongs to H because sum b_j^2/eta_j=sum eta_j theta_j2/(eta_j+lambda)2 <= (1/(4lambda))sum theta_j^2 < infinity. This also establishes existence; strict convexity gives uniqueness. f0 only needs to be in L2, not in H. The null component contributes a constant approximation error.

The empirical spectrum depends on the sample. Population eigenvalues are not known merely because a Gram matrix has been diagonalised.

The population effective dimension is \[\mathcal N(\lambda)=\sum_j\frac{\eta_j}{\eta_j+\lambda}.\] It is finite here since \(\sum_j\eta_j=\int K(x,x)\,d\rho(x)<\infty\).

Why approximate a kernel matrix?

  • Exact dense KRR becomes costly as \(n\) grows.
  • If many eigenvalues are much smaller than \(n\lambda\), they contribute little to fitted values.
  • A rank-\(r\) approximation can retain the directions most relevant at the chosen penalty.
Ideal spectral truncation

\[G_r=\sum_{j=1}^r\mu_j u_ju_j^\top,\qquad S_{\lambda,r}=\sum_{j=1}^r\frac{\mu_j}{\mu_j+n\lambda}u_ju_j^\top.\]

With eigenvalues ordered decreasingly, \(\|S_\lambda-S_{\lambda,r}\|_{\mathrm{op}} =\mu_{r+1}/(\mu_{r+1}+n\lambda)\) for \(r<n\).

Caveat: computing a full eigendecomposition first does not avoid the original dense cost.

Nyström approximation: use a small set of landmarks

Choose \(r\ll n\) landmarks \(z_1,\ldots,z_r\) and form

\[C_{ij}=K(x_i,z_j),\qquad W_{ab}=K(z_a,z_b).\]

\[\widetilde G=CW^\dagger C^\top=ZZ^\top, \qquad Z=C(W^\dagger)^{1/2}.\]

  • \(W^\dagger\) is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse; \(\widetilde G\succeq0\).
  • Fit feature ridge: \((Z^\top Z+n\lambda I_r)\beta=Z^\top y\).
  • New features are \(z(x)^\top=c(x)^\top(W^\dagger)^{1/2}\), where \(c_a(x)=K(x,z_a)\).
  • Storage is \(O(nr)\); factorisation and fitting cost \(O(nr^2+r^3)\), in addition to kernel evaluations.

This is ridge in the landmark span with the inherited RKHS norm. The projected kernel is c(x)^T Wdagger c(z). Explain Wdagger by inverting only positive eigenvalues; finite-precision implementations need an explicit threshold. Poorly chosen landmarks can miss important directions: no approximation guarantee has been claimed here. Include O(r^3) for the W eigendecomposition. One can keep only its positive eigenspace for fewer features.

5. Gaussian process regression

From random coefficients to random functions

For a fixed feature map \(\phi:\X\to\R^m\), consider

\[w\sim N(0,I_m),\qquad f(x)=\phi(x)^\top w.\]

  • Drawing \(w\) gives a whole function \(x\mapsto f(x)\).
  • Every finite collection \((f(x_1),\ldots,f(x_n))\) is jointly Gaussian.
  • The mean is zero, and \[\operatorname{Cov}(f(x),f(z)) =\phi(x)^\top\E[ww^\top]\phi(z) =\phi(x)^\top\phi(z)=K(x,z).\]

The kernel now specifies how function values co-vary under a prior distribution.

Treat the inputs as fixed. A prior is a probability distribution specified before conditioning on the observed responses. The same random coefficient vector is used at every input; drawing separate weights at each input would give a different model. Linear images of a Gaussian vector are jointly Gaussian, with Gram covariance Phi Phi^T. This recovers Bayesian feature regression before introducing an infinite collection of function values.

What is a Gaussian process?

Definition 13 (Gaussian process) A random function \(f\) is a Gaussian process, written \(f\sim\operatorname{GP}(m,K)\), if every finite vector of evaluations has distribution \[\begin{pmatrix}f(x_1)\\\vdots\\f(x_n)\end{pmatrix} \sim N\!\left( \begin{pmatrix}m(x_1)\\\vdots\\m(x_n)\end{pmatrix}, \big(K(x_i,x_j)\big)_{i,j=1}^n\right).\]

  • \(m(x)=\E[f(x)]\) is the mean function.
  • \(K(x,z)=\operatorname{Cov}(f(x),f(z))\) is the covariance kernel.
  • Its Gram matrices must be positive semidefinite: exactly our kernel condition.
  • “Gaussian” describes the joint distributions; the covariance kernel can be linear, Gaussian RBF, \(\min(s,t)\), or another valid kernel.

This is a finite-dimensional-distribution definition; do not develop the measure-theoretic construction of a stochastic process here. Singular Gaussian vectors are allowed, for example with a finite-rank kernel or repeated inputs. Symmetric positive semidefinite kernels yield consistent Gaussian finite-dimensional distributions. Smoothness of sample paths is a separate property governed by the kernel, not by the word Gaussian.

Regression with noisy observations

Use a zero-mean prior and independent observation noise:

\[f\sim\operatorname{GP}(0,K),\qquad y_i=f(x_i)+\varepsilon_i,\qquad \varepsilon_i\overset{\mathrm{iid}}\sim N(0,\sigma^2),\quad\sigma^2>0.\]

Let \(G_{ij}=K(x_i,x_j)\), \(k_*=(K(x_i,x_*))_{i=1}^n\), and \(A=G+\sigma^2I_n\). Then

Joint Gaussian distribution

\[\begin{pmatrix}y\\f(x_*)\end{pmatrix} \sim N\!\left(0, \begin{pmatrix}A&k_*\\k_*^\top&K(x_*,x_*)\end{pmatrix}\right).\]

  • The noise changes the covariance of \(y\), but not its covariance with \(f(x_*)\).
  • \(A\) is invertible even if \(G\) is singular.

Noise is independent of f as well as independent across observation indices. Even if two inputs coincide, their measurement noises are independent; the noise term belongs on the diagonal indexed by observations. For a nonzero prior mean, replace y by y-m_X and add m(x_*) to the posterior mean. The covariance formulas are unchanged.

Conditioning gives the posterior prediction

Proposition 5 (Gaussian process posterior) Given \(\mathcal D=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\) and fixed model hyperparameters, \[f(x_*)\mid\mathcal D\sim N\big(m_{\mathcal D}(x_*),s_{\mathcal D}^2(x_*)\big),\] where \[\begin{aligned} m_{\mathcal D}(x_*)&=k_*^\top A^{-1}y,\\ s_{\mathcal D}^2(x_*)&=K(x_*,x_*)-k_*^\top A^{-1}k_*. \end{aligned}\]

The posterior covariance at any two inputs is \[c_{\mathcal D}(x,z)=K(x,z)-k_x^\top A^{-1}k_z.\]

Derive the conditioning formula rather than requiring it from the inference course. Put r=f(x_)-k_^T A^{-1}y. The joint vector (r,y) is Gaussian and Cov(r,y)=k_T-k_*T A^{-1}A=0, so r is independent of y. Its mean is zero and variance is K(x_,x_)-k_^T A^{-1}k_. Conditioning on y therefore gives the displayed normal distribution. The covariance expression follows by the same residual argument at two inputs. For computation, factor A=LL^T, solve for alpha, and compute s2=K(x_,x_)-||L{-1}k_||_2^2. No explicit inverse is needed.

The connection to kernel ridge regression is exact

Compare the two predictors using the same kernel \(K\):

\[\begin{aligned} \text{GP posterior mean:}\quad &m_{\mathcal D}(x)=k_x^\top(G+\sigma^2I)^{-1}y,\\ \text{KRR predictor:}\quad &\widehat f_\lambda(x)=k_x^\top(G+n\lambda I)^{-1}y. \end{aligned}\]

Corollary 1 (GP–KRR correspondence) For the average squared loss used in this unit, \[n\lambda=\sigma^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \widehat f_\lambda=m_{\mathcal D}.\]

If the prior covariance is \(\tau^2K_0\) and KRR uses \(K_0\), the matching penalty is \(\lambda=\sigma^2/(n\tau^2)\).

Both comparisons assume zero prior mean and no unpenalised intercept. For a nonzero prior mean m, apply KRR to y-m_X and add m back. The n factor is essential because our loss is an average. The identity matches predictors for fixed hyperparameters; it does not identify Bayesian credible intervals with frequentist confidence intervals. With a fixed noise variance, the equivalent lambda changes with the sample size n.

Predict the function, or a new noisy observation?

For \(y_*=f(x_*)+\varepsilon_*\) with independent noise of variance \(\sigma^2\),

\[\operatorname{Var}(f(x_*)\mid\mathcal D)=s_{\mathcal D}^2(x_*), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(y_*\mid\mathcal D)=s_{\mathcal D}^2(x_*)+\sigma^2.\]

The bands are pointwise 95% posterior intervals, conditional on the chosen kernel and noise parameters; they are not simultaneous bands for an entire function.

Use m_D plus or minus 1.96 times the relevant posterior standard deviation. The left panel shows draws on a fixed grid, joined for visualisation. The right panel shows exact conditional marginals under the specified GP model. The observation interval retains the additional measurement-noise variance. Hyperparameter uncertainty is not integrated out. Under fixed hyperparameters the posterior variance depends on the input locations, not the observed y. That last statement changes when the hyperparameters are estimated from y.

  • A random draw from the GP need not belong to \(\H_K\).
  • Consequently, a GP prior should not be pictured as drawing a generic finite-norm element of its RKHS.

If desired, connect this distinction to the Mercer expansion. For a centred GP under the earlier compact-domain assumptions, write in L2 f=sum_j sqrt(eta_j) Z_j e_j, with independent standard normal Z_j. When there are infinitely many positive eigenvalues, RKHS membership would require sum_j Z_j^2<infinity, which fails almost surely. The posterior mean nonetheless lies in the finite training span. Do not describe an arbitrary infinite-dimensional GP using a density proportional to exp(-||f||_H^2/2) with respect to a nonexistent function-space Lebesgue measure. Finite Gaussian coefficient priors do admit the usual density and ridge/MAP argument.

Optional material

An unpenalised intercept

If an intercept is desired, fit \[\min_{b\in\R,\,f\in\H}\frac1n\|y-b\mathbf1-f_X\|_2^2+\lambda\hnorm f^2.\]

Let \(H=I-\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top/n\), \(G_c=HGH\), and \(y_c=Hy\). Then

\[\alpha=(G_c+n\lambda I)^{-1}y_c,\qquad b=\overline y-\frac1n\mathbf1^\top G\alpha, \qquad \widehat y(x)=b+k_x^\top\alpha.\]

  • \(\mathbf1^\top\alpha=0\); the equivalent centred-feature prediction is \(\overline y+k_{c,x}^\top\alpha\).
  • Centre using training means, including when predicting new inputs.
  • Merely adding a constant kernel generally penalises its coefficient.

From the optimality conditions choose alpha with (G+n lambda I)alpha+b1=y and 1^T alpha=0. Applying H gives (HGH+n lambda I)alpha=Hy. Conversely the displayed b reconstructs the first equation, so this proves the formula even for singular G. With k_{c,x}=H(k_x-G1/n), the centred prediction agrees with b+k_x^T alpha. The penalty makes f unique and the loss then makes b unique, even if constant functions also lie in H. This is for a fixed intercept, not an empirical claim about selected hyperparameters.

Random Fourier features for the Gaussian kernel

For \(\omega\sim N(0,\ell^{-2}I_d)\) and \(b\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,2\pi]\), independent,

\[\E[2\cos(\omega^\top x+b)\cos(\omega^\top z+b)] =e^{-\|x-z\|^2/(2\ell^2)}.\]

Draw \(r\) independent pairs and use

\[\phi_r(x)=\sqrt{\frac2r} \big(\cos(\omega_1^\top x+b_1),\ldots,\cos(\omega_r^\top x+b_r)\big)^\top.\]

  • \(\phi_r(x)^\top\phi_r(z)\) is an unbiased kernel approximation at fixed \(x,z\).
  • Each draw gives a valid finite-rank kernel; fit ordinary feature ridge.
  • Kernel unbiasedness does not imply unbiased fitted predictions, since solving a regularised system is nonlinear in its matrix.

Optional extension if students want a simulation-based alternative. Average over b first to obtain cos(omega^T(x-z)); then use the Gaussian characteristic function. Features must be drawn once and reused for every training and test observation. This is the construction of Rahimi and Recht (2007).
